Why Existing Law Is Insufficient

Current heritage protection frameworks target the extraction point—the moment a panel is removed from its site. This is where prosecution typically focuses:

The prosecution strategy: Charge the local extractor with theft or trafficking, attempt recovery, impose penalties. This appears to address the problem at its source.

The failure: Extraction is economically rational only because the downstream network exists. Remove panels and have no market to sell them into—extraction becomes economically irrational. But dealers, auction houses, and private collectors sustain the market. They are rarely prosecuted.

The result: Even when local extractors are convicted, theft continues because the economic incentive structure remains intact. The downstream network ensures there is always a buyer for stolen rock art.

Latour's Controversy Mapping: Following the Full Chain

Latour's method of controversy mapping traces what he calls "the full path of delegation"—every actor (human and non-human) that constitutes an action. In the context of rock art theft:

Step 1: Extraction from Site

A panel is removed. This action requires knowledge of valuable sites, physical tools, and willingness to break law. The extractor acts.

Step 2: Transit & Smuggling

Panels must move across borders. This requires routes, contacts, transportation infrastructure. Smugglers and transit personnel act. Customs evasion or corruption enables movement.

Step 3: Intermediary Dealers

Dealers purchase from smugglers or local intermediaries. They assess market value, manage inventory, create provenance stories (often false). Dealers are crucial: they transform the object from extracted material into tradeable commodity.

Step 4: Auction Houses

Major auction houses (Christie's, Sotheby's, etc.) legitimate stolen rock art by including it in catalogues. Auction houses perform critical function: they verify (often falsely) provenance and confer market value. Their authentication makes theft profitable.

Step 5: Private Collectors

Wealthy collectors are the market. They purchase without rigorous due diligence or actively avoid investigation. Their demand sustains the chain. Collection often occurs in Gulf states or private Western collections where oversight is minimal.

Each Actor in the Chain Has Agency

ANT's symmetry principle applies: each node in the chain is an actant with agency and power. To interrupt theft, all nodes must be addressed:

Extractors (Local) Power: Direct access, knowledge. Vulnerability: Economically marginal, easily prosecuted, replaceable.
Smugglers/Transit Power: Network knowledge, border crossing. Vulnerability: Often arrested but rarely convicted; supply always available.
Intermediary Dealers Power: Market knowledge, capital, laundering expertise. Vulnerability: Operate in shadow economy; difficult to trace.
Auction Houses Power: Legitimation, market-making, false provenance certification. Vulnerability: Operate publicly; subject to regulation but rarely prosecuted.
Private Collectors Power: Wealth, demand. Vulnerability: Wealthy; operate in private collections; often have diplomatic immunity or secrecy protections.

Key insight: Prosecution of extractors alone is structurally ineffective because it leaves the profitable market intact. Until dealers, auction houses, and collectors face legal accountability, theft will continue.

What Controversy Mapping Produces

The research implements Protocol P4 (Controversy Mapping & Collective Intervention) to produce:

Case-by-Case Dossiers

For 4–5 documented theft incidents, the research traces the full chain from extraction site to current location. Each dossier identifies:

• When and where extraction occurred (site documentation)
• Who extracted and sold initially (community testimony)
• Intermediary dealers (market tracing)
• Provenance falsification (document analysis)
• Auction house handling (catalogue records)
• Current owner/location (investigative research)

Prosecution-Ready Format

Dossiers are formatted for INTERPOL's Works of Art unit and national prosecutors. They provide the evidentiary chain needed to prosecute not only the extractor but intermediaries and auction houses under international trafficking law (UNESCO 1970 Convention, UNIDROIT 1995 Convention).

Accountability Mapping

For each node in the chain, the research identifies: Who profits? Who has legal standing? Who can be prosecuted under what laws? Where are enforcement gaps?

Why Communities Must Lead the Mapping

This is where PAR's epistemic sovereignty becomes essential. Communities know:

• Which panels were stolen and when. Communities hold oral histories of extraction that official records miss. They know the exact moments loss occurred.

• Where local extractors sold the panels. Extractors are often community members. Interviews conducted within community trust reveal chains of sale that external investigators cannot access.

• What provenance stories are false. When an auction house claims a panel has "obscure private collection" provenance, community members know this is false because they know the panel's actual history.

Participatory controversy mapping means communities co-produce the chain, not researchers. The research provides the ANT framework and legal expertise; communities provide the knowledge.

Legal Implications & Policy Change

1. Extended accountability: Rather than prosecuting only extractors, the chain analysis enables prosecution of dealers, auction houses, and collectors who knowingly traffic in stolen rock art or facilitate provenance fraud.

2. Auction house liability: Dossiers demonstrate that major auction houses knowingly sell stolen heritage without proper due diligence. This supports calls for strengthened regulations and potential criminal prosecution.

3. International cooperation: Tracing theft chains across multiple countries enables INTERPOL and national authorities to coordinate prosecution and recover stolen objects from current collections.

4. Structural prevention: By making the full chain visible and accountable, the research demonstrates where the system is vulnerable and what enforcement would be most effective.

Transferability: Rock Art Trafficking is Global

The same controversy chain operates for Indigenous sites globally. Arctic rock art, Andean petroglyphs, Aboriginal sacred sites—all follow similar trafficking patterns. The auction houses, dealers, and collectors are often the same networks.

What the research produces is transferable: A methodology for community-led controversy mapping that can be applied across heritage theft contexts. The specific dossiers are context-specific; the mapping methodology is global.
