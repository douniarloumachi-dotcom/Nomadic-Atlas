Neidich Cognitive Mapping — Institutional Capture vs. Community Control
How cognitive capitalism operates across Guattari's three ecologies—and where PAR-ANT restitution becomes possible

The Problem: Cognitive Capitalism & Epistemic Substitution

Warren Neidich argues that under cognitive capitalism, communities' ways of knowing are progressively replaced by externally legible representations. This happens before physical theft:

UNESCO heritage categories replace internally meaningful classifications. Panels become "Neolithic art" or "rock paintings" rather than cosmological markers or navigation anchors.

Art market valuations reframe rock art as collectible commodity. Economic value becomes the only intelligible register, replacing spiritual or practical significance.

Tourism narratives aestheticize rock art as scenic resources, erasing their function in indigenous knowledge systems. The panels become photographs to be consumed rather than cognitive infrastructure to be used.

By the time a panel is physically stolen, its community has already lost cognitive authority over it. The void site is the endpoint of a process that begins with representational capture.

Guattari's Three Ecologies: Inseparable Registers of Harm

Guattari (1989) insists that environmental, social, and mental ecologies are mutually entangled. When rock art theft occurs, harm cascades across all three simultaneously:

Environmental Ecology

Landscape features, material actants, gueltas, migration routes. Theft disrupts these. A panel that oriented routes loses that function. Water sources lose their markers. The physical landscape becomes harder to navigate.

Social Ecology

Routes, practices, governance structures, collective decision-making. When landscape anchors disappear, the social assemblies that depended on them fragment. Governance becomes harder to coordinate. Collective memory-making practices lose their infrastructure.

Mental/Cognitive Ecology

Cosmology, memory, meaning, intergenerational transmission. Knowledge that lived in panels cannot be recovered by documentation alone. Elders report navigational uncertainty. Young people cannot learn routes as their ancestors did. The cognitive architecture dissolves.

Response to any single register will fail. Legal proceedings addressing only property (environmental theft). UNESCO interventions addressing only cultural value (mental ecology). Development projects addressing only social infrastructure (routes). But these are inseparable. Effective heritage protection must operate across all three simultaneously.

Cognitive Mapping: Making Capture Visible

Neidich's cognitive mapping makes institutional capture spatially legible as a political argument. In this research, we map institutional capture across two dimensions:

Vertical axis: Degree of institutional capture

Bottom (⬇): Knowledge fully under community control. Vocabulary, meaning, and governance are internally determined.

Top (⬆): Knowledge fully captured or substituted by external institutions. UNESCO categories dominate. Market valuations determine significance. Tourism narratives are the public register.

Horizontal axis: Guattari's three ecologies

Left: Environmental ecology — landscape, material actants, water sources, atmospheric phenomena.

Centre: Social ecology — routes, practices, governance, collective decision-making.

Right: Mental/Cognitive ecology — cosmology, memory, meaning, intergenerational transmission.

The map then plots specific actants and knowledge domains across these two axes, making visible where communities have retained epistemic sovereignty and where external capture has occurred.

Reading the Cognitive Map: Dashed vs. Solid Arrows

Dashed Arrows (Upward) = Extraction & Substitution

These trace the movement of knowledge away from community control toward institutional capture. Cognitive capitalism moves knowledge upward. External representations substitute for internal meaning. This is the process of epistemic dispossession.

Solid Arrows (Downward) = PAR-ANT Restitution

These trace the research's interventions pulling knowledge back down: reclaiming vocabulary, co-producing governance tools, returning epistemic authority to communities. The green arrows show where PAR-ANT works.

Key insight: The research cannot recover stolen panels. But it can restore community epistemic authority over what remains by working downward—asserting cognitive sovereignty against institutional capture.

The Void Site in Cognitive Space

On the cognitive map, the void site sits top-left—fully captured by the market, removed from community control. It is simultaneously extracted from the landscape (environmental ecology) and absent from community knowledge production (mental ecology).

The green downward arrows show where research intervenes: asserting community epistemic authority over the disrupted routes, cosmologies, and governance practices that the void left behind.

This is not recovery. It is epistemic restitution—the restoration of community authority to interpret and govern what the theft left behind. The void remains absent. But the community's right to speak about that absence, and to govern knowledge production around it, is restored.

Operationalising Cognitive Mapping: Research Integration

Protocol P1 (Participatory Assemblage Mapping) begins the cognitive mapping process: communities identify which actants matter, across all three ecologies. This is the baseline for measuring capture.

Protocol P2 (Co-Analysis Interviews) makes institutional capture explicitly discussable. Questions like "What do outsiders call this? What do you call it?" map the gap between external representation and internal meaning. Untranslatable concepts mark points where community language has resisted external substitution.

Neidich's mapping then visualizes these tensions, showing on the two-dimensional plane where each knowledge domain sits relative to institutional capture and ecological register.

The result: Communities see where their epistemic sovereignty has been eroded and where it remains intact. The map becomes a governance tool for identifying which domains most urgently need protection and restitution.

Transferability: Cognitive Capitalism is Structural, Not Regional

The same substitution dynamic operates globally. Sámi herding knowledge captured by climate science categories. Aboriginal sacred sites aestheticized by tourism. Indigenous Andean astronomy replaced by Western classifications.

Neidich's cognitive mapping translates across these contexts because the structure of cognitive capitalism is the same: external representation replaces internal meaning; institutional frameworks dominate local epistemologies; knowledge becomes capital.

Each nomadic community will have its own pattern of capture and resistance. The mapping methodology is transferable. The specific configurations are not.

Why Cognitive Mapping Matters for Heritage Protection

1. Prevention before extraction. By identifying and interrupting epistemic capture, communities can resist the process that makes physical theft economically viable. If rock art retains local meaning and governance, it is less valuable to external markets.

2. Accountability to institutional capture. UNESCO, auction houses, and collectors are actants in cognitive capitalism. Mapping them reveals where external capture is happening and who benefits from it.

3. Governance interventions at multiple levels. Cognitive maps show where institutional partnerships are possible (domains where capture is already deep) and where community authority is strongest (domains most worth protecting).

4. Measurable restitution. Unlike cultural recovery narratives, cognitive mapping makes visible whether communities are regaining epistemic authority over their own knowledge—whether the downward arrows are actually moving the map.
